About the AI

How our AI works, and what it can't do

A plain-English look at how the AI was built and tested, so you know how much to trust a result.

What it does

{{ brand }} has two AI checkers. You choose the one that fits your photo:

  • Mole or spot: compares a close-up photo of one spot with {{ by_domain['lesion']|length }} types of moles and skin spots, including skin cancers.
  • Rash or other skin problem: compares an everyday phone photo with {{ by_domain['general']|length }} common conditions such as eczema, hives, fungal infections, acne and shingles.

Each checker gives a score for every condition it knows, and the scores add up to 100%. We only name one condition when its score is high enough and clearly ahead of the rest. Otherwise we tell you the AI isn't sure.

What it learned from

Mole or spot: HAM10000, a free public collection of 10,015 close-up skin photos taken with a skin magnifier (dermatoscope) at a hospital in Vienna, Austria, and a skin clinic in Queensland, Australia. More than half were confirmed by a lab test (biopsy).

Tschandl P., Rosendahl C., Kittler H. The HAM10000 dataset. Scientific Data 5, 180161 (2018). doi:10.1038/sdata.2018.161 · CC BY-NC 4.0.

Rash or other skin problem: SCIN (Skin Condition Image Network) from Google Research: phone photos shared by volunteers in the USA in 2023, each looked at by up to three dermatologists. We only used photos where the dermatologists mostly agreed on one condition, which gave about 1,500 cases (3,200 photos) across 10 conditions.

SCIN dataset, Google Research & Stanford Medicine · github.com/google-research-datasets/scin · SCIN Data Use License.

How it was built

  • We started from AI models that already understand everyday photos (MobileNetV2 and EfficientNetB0), then taught them about skin. For each checker we keep whichever did better.
  • Every photo is cropped to a square and resized the same way when the AI learns and when it checks your photo.
  • While learning, photos were flipped, turned and lightened or darkened, so the AI copes with different photos.
  • Common conditions get less weight and rare ones more, so the AI doesn't just guess the most common answer.
  • Photos of the same spot or person were never split between learning and testing, so test results aren't inflated.

Sensitivity = how often it finds a condition when it's really there. Specificity = how often it correctly says "not this one". These results come from photos in the same collections, are not the same as a doctor's accuracy, and real-life results will likely be lower. For the rash checker, some conditions had only about 10 test cases, so their numbers can swing a lot.

Things to keep in mind

  • It doesn't know every skin disease. It knows {{ classes|length }} common conditions. Anything else will be matched to the closest one it knows.
  • Photo type matters. The mole checker learned from magnified close-ups. The rash checker learned from normal phone photos.
  • Skin tone matters. The mole photos are mostly of lighter skin. The rash photos include more skin tones, but darker skin is still under-represented.
  • Doctors often disagree on rashes. The rash checker's training labels carry that uncertainty, so "not sure" results are more common there.
  • It is not a medical device. It hasn't been tested in real clinics.

Who it's for

People who want to learn about a skin problem and prepare for a doctor's visit, plus students and researchers. It is for non-commercial use only, and it must never replace a doctor.
